Debt Repayment Strategies in Canada: What Works Best?

If you are carrying more than one debt, the order you pay them off matters almost as much as how much you pay. Two Canadians with the same debts and the same monthly budget can finish months apart and pay hundreds of dollars more or less in interest, purely because of the order they chose.

This guide compares the main debt repayment strategies available in Canada: the debt avalanche, the debt snowball, consolidation, balance transfers, and the formal options for when repayment is no longer realistic. It includes a worked example in Canadian dollars so you can see the actual difference, and a step-by-step plan you can start this week.

Quick answer: The debt avalanche (highest interest rate first) saves the most money. The debt snowball (smallest balance first) gives you faster wins and helps many people stick with the plan. Either one beats paying only the minimums. Run your own debts through the free Credit Pulse Debt Payoff Calculator to see which one works best for you.

Step zero: list every debt you owe

Every strategy below depends on one thing: an accurate list. Before choosing a method, write down each debt with four numbers:

  • Current balance, from your latest statement or online banking.
  • Interest rate (APR). Credit cards in Canada commonly charge around 20% or more on purchases; lines of credit and car loans are usually lower.
  • Minimum or required monthly payment.
  • Due date, so you never miss a payment while you work the plan.

Include everything: credit cards, store cards, lines of credit, personal and car loans, student loans, buy now, pay later plans, and money you owe to family. A forgotten balance changes the math for every method.

Then work out how much you can put toward debt each month in total. If you are not sure, build a quick budget first with the free Credit Pulse Budget Calculator. The number left after essentials is your repayment budget.

The debt avalanche: highest interest rate first

With the avalanche method, you pay the minimum on every debt and send every extra dollar to the debt with the highest interest rate. When that one is paid off, you roll its payment into the next-highest rate, and so on.

Because the most expensive debt shrinks first, less interest builds up overall. Mathematically, the avalanche always costs the same or less than any other order.

Best for: people who are motivated by the total dollars saved and can stay patient if the first payoff takes a while.

The debt snowball: smallest balance first

With the snowball method, you pay the minimum on everything and send extra money to the debt with the smallest balance, regardless of its rate. Once it is gone, its payment rolls into the next-smallest balance, so your payment grows like a snowball.

The snowball usually costs a bit more in interest, but you close accounts sooner. Each closed account means one less bill and one less due date, and that visible progress is what keeps many people going.

Best for: people who have started and stopped debt plans before, or who feel overwhelmed by the number of bills.

Snowball vs avalanche: a worked example in CAD

Here is a realistic Canadian example. Imagine three debts with combined minimum payments of $450 a month, and a total repayment budget of $650 a month (that is, $200 extra above the minimums).

Three things stand out:

  1. Having a plan matters most. Adding $200 a month and rolling payments forward cuts nearly three years off the timeline and saves about $2,850 to $3,100 in interest, whichever order you pick.
  2. The avalanche saves about $256 more than the snowball here, because the 20.99% credit card is attacked first.
  3. The snowball delivers a win 13 months sooner. The personal loan disappears in month 7, compared with month 20 for the first payoff under the avalanche.

Debt consolidation in Canada

Debt consolidation means replacing several debts with one new loan or line of credit, ideally at a lower interest rate. Common options in Canada include a personal consolidation loan, an unsecured line of credit, or a home equity line of credit (HELOC) for homeowners.

When it helps: you qualify for a meaningfully lower rate than your current average, and one payment makes it easier to stay organized.

When it backfires: the paid-off credit cards get used again, and you end up with the new loan plus new card balances. It also does not reduce what you owe; it changes the rate and the schedule.

Before signing, compare the total cost in dollars, not just the monthly payment. A lower payment stretched over more years can cost more overall.

Balance transfer credit cards

A balance transfer moves a high-interest credit card balance to a new card with a low promotional rate for a limited time, often 6 to 12 months. There is usually a transfer fee, commonly a few percent of the amount moved.

It works only if you can pay off most or all of the transferred balance before the promotional rate ends. Read the terms carefully: the rate after the promotion, how new purchases are charged, and what happens if a payment is late.

Credit counselling and formal options

If your repayment budget cannot realistically cover your minimum payments, a self-directed plan may not be enough. Canada has structured options:

  • Non-profit credit counselling. A counsellor reviews your budget and debts and explains your options. Many non-profit agencies offer the first session free. If appropriate, they may set up a debt management plan with your creditors.
  • Consumer proposal. A legal agreement to repay part of what you owe, arranged through a Licensed Insolvency Trustee, for total debts under $250,000 (not counting your mortgage).
  • Bankruptcy. A legal process, also through a Licensed Insolvency Trustee, for people who cannot repay their debts.

Both formal options have serious, long-lasting effects on your credit, so speak with a qualified professional first. Our guide to free debt management resources in Canada explains where to find each one and how to spot debt-relief scams.

Your step-by-step debt repayment plan

  1. List every debt with its balance, rate, minimum payment and due date.
  2. Build a budget and set a fixed monthly amount for debt. The Budget Calculator helps you find it.
  3. Pick your method: avalanche if saving money matters most, snowball if you need early wins.
  4. Automate the minimums on every account so nothing is ever late.
  5. Send every extra dollar to your target debt, including tax refunds, bonuses and any one-time money.
  6. Roll payments forward as each debt is paid off; do not let the freed-up money disappear into spending.
  7. Review monthly and rerun your numbers in the Debt Payoff Calculator to see your new debt-free date.
  8. Get help early if minimums are no longer manageable. Waiting usually narrows your options.

Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to pay off debt in Canada?

Put a fixed amount above your minimums toward one debt at a time and roll each payment forward when a debt is paid off. The debt avalanche (highest interest rate first) is usually fastest and cheapest. One-time money such as a tax refund or bonus, applied to your highest-rate debt, speeds things up further.

Is the debt snowball or debt avalanche better?

The avalanche saves the most interest because it targets the highest rate first. The snowball pays off small balances first, which gives quicker wins and helps many people stick with the plan. In our worked example, the avalanche saved about $256 more, while the snowball paid off its first debt 13 months sooner.

Does debt consolidation hurt your credit score in Canada?

Applying for a consolidation loan usually involves a credit check, which can cause a small, temporary dip. Over time, consolidation can help if it lowers your interest and you make every payment on time. It can hurt if you run the paid-off cards back up.

How much should I put toward debt each month?

Start with your minimum payments, then add whatever your budget allows. The 50/30/20 rule suggests putting up to 20% of take-home pay toward savings and extra debt payments. A budget calculator shows what is realistic for you.

When should I talk to a credit counsellor?

If you are only making minimum payments and balances are not falling, if you are missing payments, or if you are using credit for essentials, contact a reputable non-profit credit counselling agency. Many offer the first session free.
